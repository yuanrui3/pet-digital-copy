Pikachu's Keep

A copy of one real cat, built only from public research data: its body and coat from a twelve-second phone video, its home from a phone scan, and its comings and goings from a month of home videos.

Loading the cat…
Pikachu's own meow, and other cats' voices for the sounds it never made on camera. At home the copy trills when it heads for the person, meows when it reaches them and purrs when it settles near them; in the body clip it meows when it turns to the camera.

Body

A 3D reconstruction (Lab4D) of a twelve-second video of the cat. For a test, the model never saw the colours of the shaded stretch of the timeline. "As trained, frame by frame" shows how its idea of the lighting drifted there; "Steady coat" uses one well-lit moment for the whole clip.

"Photo coat" is not the model's own colouring: it paints the video's pixels onto the model's surface, using the model's camera and pose for each of the 76 frames. Each spot takes its colour from the frames that saw it most head-on and agree with each other; spots the camera never saw well, such as the belly, keep the model's colours.

At home

The room is the owner's phone scan. The lines are where the cat actually went in 19 home videos over a month (19 minutes in all). The cat here is Agent-to-Sim's reconstruction of Pikachu from that whole month, and it moves its body exactly as it did on camera: eating, sitting by the litter box, curling up on the sofa. The small orange phone marks the person filming.

The copy on its own

In these walks nothing is replayed: a behaviour model (Agent-to-Sim's, trained here on 17 of the 19 videos) picks where the cat goes next, 5.6 s at a time, and how its body moves, for about a minute. Each walk starts from a real moment in one of the two videos it never trained on. Asked to predict those two videos 5.6 s ahead, it placed the route about twice as close as replaying the cat's most similar recorded moments (0.16 m off against 0.35 m), but its guesses of where the cat ends up bunched together and were barely better (0.78 m against 0.79 m).

So these walks use a changed goal choice, with no retraining: every 5.6 s, a quarter of the time the copy heads for one of the spots where the real cat often ended up (within 3 m of where it is), otherwise for a place of its own choosing. A quarter was picked so that the walks move as much as the real cat does in the videos it trained on: 0.56 m per 5.6 s on average, against the real cat's 0.54 m (heading for a spot half the time made them move 0.86 m; never, 0.23 m). Scored on the two unseen videos, the best of its 16 guesses landed 0.57 m from where the cat really went, against 0.78 m before and 0.76 m for guessing the cat's usual spots alone, and it also did better on a score that does not reward scattered guesses. The walks under "Where you put the person" and "Calling the cat" keep the old goal choice, since their results were measured with it.

Where you put the person

Press "Place the person" and click near one of the 12 rings, the places where people really stood while filming. The copy then walks for 34 s with the person standing there; where the person is, is the only thing it reacts to. Under "The copy beside the real cat", the copy and the real cat (pale) start together in the two videos it never trained on, with the person moving as they really did.

It does react: moving the person to the opposite side of the cat changes where the copy heads by about 0.57 m, against 0.02 m between two random draws, and removing the person changes it by 0.46 m. But it does not simply head for the person: after the move it shifts toward the person's new side only 59% of the time, and it ends up nearer the person 41% of the time, about as often as the real cat did (38%). The person filming usually follows the cat, so the model may have learned where the person goes rather than how the cat responds.

Calling the cat

Press "Call the cat" and click one of the 12 blue spots, places where the real cat often was (on the floor or up on furniture). The copy is given that spot as its goal and gets there by its own route and gait, then carries on by itself.

What else it reacts to: switching off its view of the room shifts where it heads by about 0.12 m, against 0.02 m between two random draws; yet its goals land where the real cat has been almost as often without the room (87%) as with it (90%). A person walking matters far more: compared with the same person walking away, the copy's goal moves about a metre, and in 4 of 5 moments an approaching person pushes it away. That may be the filming habit again: the person walked toward the cat mostly when it was leaving, and the model cannot tell the two apart.

The grey and white cat at home

Pick it under "Cat" in the At home bar. It has no home videos of its own, so it borrows Pikachu's: the recorded month and the copy's walks. It keeps its own body, coat and skinning; only the motion is Pikachu's.

The two models' skeletons have the same 25 joints, but both were learnt freely, so their bones do not move the same body parts: the grey cat's "left hand" bone moves its right front leg, for example. So each of the grey cat's bones follows Pikachu's flesh rather than Pikachu's bone of the same name. Its body (posed as it stood in one frame of its own video, the one closest to Pikachu's standing pose) is lined up with Pikachu's and gently bent onto it, with paws, nose and tail tip pinned, to find which part of Pikachu each part of it corresponds to. Each of its bones then moves rigidly with the blend of Pikachu's bones that move that part. Each frame it is raised or lowered so its lowest point sits where Pikachu's does (its legs are shorter, so it would otherwise sink about 3 cm).

It moves as Pikachu does, in its own slimmer shape, so its poses are close to Pikachu's but not exact. Beside a replay, the pale cat is still the real Pikachu.
